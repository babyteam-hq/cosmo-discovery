# Proposed test: why do Milky Way satellites sit near their pericentres? Selection against simulation

Question: Gaia proper motions show that many Milky Way satellite galaxies are currently near the pericentres of their orbits, more than random orbital phases would give. Is this an observational selection effect (satellites near pericentre are closer and brighter, so easier to discover), a feature of the potential, or a sign of something about their accretion? Measure the near-pericentre fraction from Gaia DR3 orbits and compare it with the same fraction in simulated satellite populations passed through the real discovery selection functions.

Data: Gaia DR3 proper motions of Milky Way satellites (published compilations), their distances and line-of-sight velocities; public Milky Way potential models; public simulated satellite catalogues (e.g. from cosmological zoom-in suites with released subhalo catalogues); the published DES, Pan-STARRS and SDSS satellite detection-efficiency functions.

Method: fixed before computing: the satellite sample (distance, luminosity cuts), the potentials (two or three, including one with the LMC), the statistic (fraction with orbital phase within a fixed interval of pericentre), the simulated sample (all subhalos above a stated mass, observed from a solar position and passed through the detection-efficiency functions), and the comparison (the observed fraction against the distribution over simulated observers). Interval from orbit uncertainties (Monte Carlo over the observables) and over simulated hosts.

Expected precision: about 40-50 satellites with good orbits; the fraction to about +-0.07; enough to tell whether selection accounts for the excess.
